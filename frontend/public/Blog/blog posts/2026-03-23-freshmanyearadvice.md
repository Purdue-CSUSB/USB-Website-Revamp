Understandably, the shift from high school to college can be overwhelming. Suddenly, classrooms filled with 30 familiar faces turn into lecture halls of 300 strangers. In college, a few midterms and finals determine your grade in a course far more than the hours spent completing assignments. High school teachers get to know you, while professors don’t even encounter some of their students face-to-face. On top of that, college content is tough, and the phrase “I’ll never need this knowledge in real life” isn’t quite true.

The learning opportunities and environment provided by higher education can seem scary, but it’s just that college requires a different approach than high school. As a senior reflecting on my experience at Purdue, I’ve been evaluating the habits that have allowed me to succeed, and those that are unproductive. As such, I will offer some of my best advice for succeeding as a freshman in CS at Purdue University. 

1. Start Early.

Even if you don’t have a procrastination problem, it’s easy to underestimate how long an assignment will take - especially in computer science. I think it’s fair to say that all CS majors have encountered a similar scenario: You work on a coding assignment that is due the next day, and you unexpectedly encounter a bug that you can’t fix immediately. Suddenly, you are pulling an all-nighter trying to complete your work. Especially at the beginning of your CS learning journey, it can be hard to anticipate the technical issues you might face. For this reason, it is crucial to start assignments shortly after they are released. Assume that a task will take longer than you initially think it will and set aside time accordingly. This gives you more time, should you need it, to comfortably fix issues in your code and ask TAs and professors for help. An organizational tip that has helped me with this is inputting all of my assignments into a calendar, and recording the due dates as a few days before the assignment is officially due. For example, if an assignment is due on Friday, I would add it to my calendar as being due Wednesday. This gives you a buffer for any unexpected challenges. It’ll save you the frustration, stress, sleepless nights, and dents in your grade percentage.


2. Do your homework with the goal of mastery and understanding, not for completion.

Studying for midterms and finals in college is a lot more work than preparing for exams in high school. They cover far more content, and if you are taking multiple challenging classes, it is not feasible to prepare for several exams at once. Therefore, it is best to retain as much content as possible during the semester, rather than cramming everything a week before an exam. For example, while completing homework, make sure that you are understanding the fundamental concepts, rather than mindlessly following the assignment instructions. Ask yourself questions, such as “Why exactly am I using this principle?” and “What if I had approached this problem differently? Would it lead to the same result?” Do your work with purpose. Put the concepts into your long-term memory so that you don’t have to spend your study sessions relearning content. Your time is better spent practicing test questions. 
Here are some examples of what this could look like in your CS classes:
CS182: When you go to office hours for completing homework, not only should you ask questions about the problems you are struggling with, but also those you feel that you already understand decently. Explain to the TA your approach to solving the problem, and ask them if your reasoning is correct. If you have minor flaws in your answer, then you can learn from your mistakes - which would not have happened if you had not asked because you were confident in your initial work. Additionally, you might learn a new approach to solving the problem. 
CS240: Write out your functions on paper before coding in vim. This is a strategy that is strongly encouraged by CS240 professors. This allows you to think about all aspects of your code, rather than trying various solutions and comparing them against the test module. Also, the exams for this class are handwritten, so you will get more practice throughout the semester of writing accurate code on paper.


3. It never, ever, hurts to ask for extra help.

As a high school student, I was sometimes hesitant to express my doubts about class content because I was afraid that a classmate or instructor may judge me or question my abilities. Also, I would refrain from asking for a gentle extension regardless of my workload. Do not get trapped in this self-sabotaging behavior. Because of the large number of students in the CS program, most courses have several resources for getting help, such as lab sessions, TA/professor office hours, and PSOs. Use these resources early and often. Instead of spending hours trying to figure out your problem on your own, you could get help from your TAs and professors. If you don’t ask for help because you are afraid of being judged, you may never fully understand a concept. If you try to break down that fear, not only will you become a better communicator, but you will also understand the class content much more thoroughly. Besides, most people are more than happy to offer assistance. Articulate your needs, and don’t face your academic challenges alone.

4. Don’t be intimidated. 

Freshmen enter the program with varying experiences in computer science. Some students come in with no background, others may have taken basic CS classes, and some have already started building advanced projects. Having limited coding experience does NOT mean that you are behind or somehow not as smart as the rest of your class. If you put in the work, you are capable of doing well in your classes. Instead of comparing yourself to your peers and viewing them as competition, study with them and use each other as inspiration. Build yourself a community.
